FAILURE MECHANISMS
Do the reasons a language model fails have anything to do with why it
actually fails?

A plain-language guide to the failure_mechanisms experiment: the question,
the six things it needs to be true, how the pipeline tests them, and what
each possible result would mean.


THE PROBLEM, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
------------------------------
When a language model gets a math problem wrong, you can ask another model
to explain what went wrong, and it will happily tell you: "it applied the
discount to the wrong quantity," or "it dropped a step in the middle."
These explanations sound reasonable. But there's a real possibility they're
just plausible-sounding stories - a fluent description of the outward
behavior that has no actual connection to what happened inside the network.
The model produced the wrong number; a second model produced a
wrong-sounding story to match. Neither one tells you anything about the
mechanism.

This experiment asks a narrower, checkable version of that worry: when two
failures get the same human-readable label, do they also share a distinct,
reproducible pattern inside the model's computation - something you can
find by directly intervening on its internal activations - or is the label
just a coincidence of phrasing?


SIX QUESTIONS THE PIPELINE HAS TO ANSWER
------------------------------------------
The central question above only means something if it's built on solid
ground. The pipeline breaks it into six checkable pieces, roughly in the
order the stages actually test them.

01. Can "why it failed" even be measured consistently?
    If two independent judge models can't agree on a description of the
    same failure, there's no stable label to test in the first place.

02. Does a failure's label predict its internal signature?
    The main test: failures sharing a semantic category should look more
    alike, mechanistically, than failures picked at random - more than
    chance, and more than the problem's own difficulty would explain.

03. Does that signature add anything real?
    If the internal signature only tells you what you could already guess
    from surface features (problem length, edit size, and so on), it isn't
    carrying new information.

04. Does it hold at every level of detail?
    Categories can be coarse ("arithmetic error") or fine ("misapplied a
    percentage to the wrong operand"). A real effect should survive
    zooming in and out.

05. Would the categories re-emerge on their own?
    Cluster the internal signatures with no knowledge of the human labels
    at all - do the resulting groups line up with the taxonomy, or invent
    a different one?

06. Does it matter practically, and does it replicate?
    If a fix that resolves one failure of a given type also resolves a
    different failure of the SAME type more often than a mismatched one -
    and the whole pattern shows up again on a second, untouched benchmark
    - that's a much stronger claim than a one-off correlation.


THE METHOD, IN SIX MOVES
--------------------------
1. Start from a benchmark the model mostly gets right
   Grade-school math word problems (GSM8K). The model solves the great
   majority of them correctly - that's the point: failures are the
   interesting minority, not the norm.

2. Nudge each problem without changing its meaning
   Swap a name, reformat a number, reword a sentence - tiny edits that
   shouldn't change the correct answer at all.

3. Find where the model flips
   For a lot of problems, the model answers every variant correctly.
   Occasionally, one specific rewording tips it into a wrong answer. That
   pair - right on variant A, wrong on variant B, same underlying problem
   - isolates one clean failure from everything else that could be going
   on.

4. Ask independently what went wrong
   Two smaller models, unrelated to each other and to the model being
   studied, each read the failed reasoning and write a short description -
   deliberately kept blind to anything about internal activations.

5. Find where it went wrong, mechanically
   Separately and without looking at the descriptions: swap the model's
   internal layer-by-layer activations between the right run and the wrong
   run, one layer at a time, and measure which swaps actually flip the
   outcome. That produces a numeric "causal signature" for the failure - a
   profile of which layers mattered.

6. Test whether the two sides agree
   Statistics ask: do failures with the same human-written label also have
   more similar causal signatures than failures with different labels -
   after accounting for everything the problem and the edit itself could
   already explain?


EVERY STAGE OF THE PIPELINE
------------------------------
Seventeen scripts, run in order. [GATE] marks a reliability gate - a hard
checkpoint the run isn't allowed to quietly skip past.

Phase 0 - choosing a model
  00_pilot          Model pilot
                     Runs a handful of flips through each candidate model,
                     purely to check the measurement will actually work -
                     flip rate, trace quality, non-degenerate patching
                     signal. Selection happens on measurement grounds
                     only, before any result is visible.

Phase 1 - generating the data
  01 variants        Make surface variants
                      Applies the name-swap / number-format / wording
                      perturbations to a batch of problems.
  02 traces           Generate reasoning traces
                      Runs every variant through the model and records its
                      full step-by-step answer - the biggest single time
                      cost in the whole pipeline.
  03 pairs            Select clean/fail pairs
                      Picks out the problems where one variant was solved
                      and a near-identical one wasn't.
  04 stable           Build stable controls
                      Also keeps correct-to-correct pairs, later used as a
                      baseline so "the failure's signature" means excess
                      signal beyond ordinary variation.

Phase 2 - describing the failures
  05 annotate [GATE, feeds Gate B]   Two independent judges
                      Each judge model reads a failure and writes a short,
                      structured description of the first observable
                      error - blind to the other judge and to any internal
                      activations.
  06 reliability [GATE]              Semantic reliability - Gate B
                      Do the two judges agree with each other? Measured
                      with Cohen's kappa and Krippendorff's alpha. If they
                      don't agree, there's no stable category to test
                      downstream, and the run should stop here rather than
                      build a taxonomy on noise.
  07 taxonomy         Freeze the taxonomy
                      Clusters the surviving descriptions into a taxonomy
                      of failure types and locks it - every later stage
                      uses this exact taxonomy, so results can't be
                      quietly re-tuned after the fact.

Phase 3 - finding the mechanism
  08 exact patch      Exact activation patching
                      The core measurement: for each pair, swap
                      activations layer by layer and record the effect,
                      then subtract the stable-pair baseline to get each
                      failure's causal signature.
  09 attribution      Attribution patching
                      A faster, gradient-based estimate of the same thing,
                      computed independently on a subset - a cross-check,
                      not a replacement.
  10 reliability [GATE]               Mechanistic reliability - Gate C
                      Do the exact and gradient-based estimates agree with
                      each other more than two unrelated failures agree by
                      chance? If the measurement itself isn't reliable,
                      nothing built on top of it can be trusted.

Phase 4 - the actual test
  11 correspondence   Main correspondence test
                      The confirmatory test: do same-category pairs have
                      more similar causal signatures than different-
                      category pairs, beyond chance? Answers question 02.
  12 prediction       Incremental prediction test
                      Does adding the causal signature improve prediction
                      of failure type beyond surface covariates alone?
                      Answers question 03.
  13 resolution       Resolution sweep
                      Repeats the correspondence test at coarse, medium,
                      and fine-grained taxonomy levels. Answers question
                      04.
  14 split/merge      Blind re-clustering
                      Clusters the causal signatures with no knowledge of
                      the taxonomy, then compares the two groupings.
                      Answers question 05.

Phase 5 & 6 - does it matter, does it hold up
  15 transfer         Intervention transfer
                      Tests whether an intervention location that fixes
                      one failure also helps a different failure of the
                      same type more often than a mismatched one - a
                      functional, not just statistical, check.
  16 replicate        Frozen replication on SVAMP
                      Re-runs the whole measurement on a second, untouched
                      benchmark using the taxonomy and thresholds exactly
                      as frozen - no re-tuning allowed. Answers question
                      06.


WHAT THE RESULT COULD ACTUALLY LOOK LIKE
--------------------------------------------
This isn't a pass/fail experiment - it can land in a few genuinely
different places, and each one means something different.

[STRONG POSITIVE] Semantic labels are mechanistically grounded
  Same-label failures cluster together causally, the signature improves
  prediction beyond covariates, the effect survives at every taxonomy
  resolution, blind clustering rediscovers the categories on its own,
  fixes transfer within a category more than across categories, and the
  whole thing replicates on SVAMP. This would be real evidence that
  today's semantic failure-mode language tracks something the model is
  actually doing - not just a story.

[MIXED RESULT] Real, but coarser than expected
  Correspondence holds at coarse categories ("arithmetic slip") but breaks
  down at fine-grained ones ("misapplied percentage to wrong operand").
  This would suggest the categories people actually use are meaningfully
  real, but finer-grained taxonomies currently used in interpretability
  write-ups may be over-claiming precision the mechanism doesn't support.

[NULL RESULT] The labels are just plausible stories
  Same-label failures are no more mechanistically similar than randomly
  matched ones, once covariates are accounted for. A real, useful negative
  result - it would mean a common interpretability practice (asking a
  model to explain its own or another model's mistake) is producing
  fluent narrative, not causal insight, at least for this kind of failure.

[INCONCLUSIVE] The question goes unanswered
  Any of the four gates fails - judges don't agree, the two patching
  methods don't agree, or the sample is too small for the statistics to
  mean anything. This isn't evidence against the hypothesis; it means the
  measurement wasn't sound enough to test it, and the honest move is to
  say so rather than report a number anyway.

Where this run actually stands: it's a small end-to-end test (--target-n
40 against the design's own recommended minimum of 120-200), being run to
prove the pipeline works mechanically before committing a much longer run
at full scale. At this size, the INCONCLUSIVE outcome above is the
expected one, not a failure of the experiment.
